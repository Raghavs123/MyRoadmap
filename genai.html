Part A — Foundations

1 What is Generative AI? (the 10-second answer)

Generative AI is AI that makes new things — text, pictures, voices, music, videos, computer code, 3D shapes — that didn't exist before, by learning the patterns in huge amounts of examples and then producing fresh work in the same style.
Imagine a child who has looked at a million drawings of cats. Ask them to "draw a cat wearing a tiny hat" and they can — they've never seen that exact picture, but they know what cats look like and what hats look like, and can combine the ideas. Generative AI is that child, at enormous scale.

Three words capture it: learn the pattern → start from a prompt → produce something new. You type a request (the prompt); the model makes an answer that has never been written, painted or filmed before.

You give it…It gives you…Everyday example
A sentenceA paragraph, email, story, or program"Write a thank-you note to my teacher"
A sentenceA picture"A red panda reading a book in a cozy library"
A picture + a sentenceAn edited picture"Make the sky sunset-orange"
A sentenceA video clip, maybe with sound"A paper boat sailing down a rainy street"

A photoA 3D scene you can walk aroundTurn photos of a room into a 3D model

Chat assistants like Claude or ChatGPT are generative AI for text; the deep-dive on how they work is the LLM guide. This guide is the whole family — and especially the picture, sound and video side.

2 Why is it different? Making vs sorting

For many years, most AI was discriminative — it sorted or judged things: "is this email spam?", "is this photo a cat or a dog?". Generative AI does the opposite job: it makes the cat.

Fig 1 — Discriminative models turn things into labels; generative models turn descriptions into things.
Discriminative AIGenerative AI
Core question"Which category is this?""What would a new example look like?"

The two work together all the time: a generative model drafts, a discriminative model checks (is the picture safe? does it match the prompt? is the answer correct?).

Part B — How It Works

6 How a Model Learns to "Make Things"

There is one big idea hiding behind every generative model: learn what real examples look like, so you can produce new ones that look real too. Mathematicians say the model learns the distribution of the data — the way the examples are spread out. You don't need the math to follow the picture:

Picture a huge field, and every real cat photo ever taken is a flag planted in it. Photos of similar cats are planted close together; very different photos are far apart. The model learns the shape of where the flags are. To make a new cat, it picks a spot in the flag-filled region that nobody has used yet. Because the spot is near many real cats, the result looks like a cat.

Three kinds of "learning games" the models play

GameThe ruleUsed by
🔮 Guess the next pieceSee the start; predict what comes next. Repeat many times.Text models, and image/audio models that work in tokens
🧽 Clean up the noiseTake a real example, smear it with noise, learn to undo the damage.Diffusion and flow models (most image/video/audio)
🎭 Fool the judgeOne network forges, another tries to spot fakes; both get better.GANs
None of them stores a picture album to copy from. They store dials (numbers) tuned so that realistic things come out. (Under special conditions a model can still memorize a few training examples — see Section 41.)

7 Latent Space — The Map of Ideas

A 1000×1000 photo has a million pixel values — far too many to "think" about directly. So models learn to squeeze a picture into a short list of numbers capturing what matters ("a fluffy orange cat, sitting, facing left, indoors") and to expand it back. That squeezed-down space is the latent space ("latent" = hidden).

Fig 3 — The encoder/decoder pair compresses pictures to a compact "idea space" and back. Working there is much cheaper than working on raw pixels.
It's like describing a person to a sketch artist: you don't send a million measurements — you say "tall, curly hair, round glasses, big smile". Those few words are the latent code; the artist (decoder) turns them into a face.
  • Why it matters: most modern image and video generators run their expensive steps in latent space, which is why they became fast enough to use (Section 13).
  • Why it's fun: you can blend ideas — "half cat, half owl" — by mixing latent codes; and move along directions such as "older", "smiling" or "more sunny".
  • Related: word embeddings from the LLM guide are the same idea for text.

9 Autoregressive Models — One Piece at a Time

"Autoregressive" is a fancy word for "predict the next piece from everything so far, then add it and repeat." That's exactly how language models write. It works for anything you can cut into a sequence of pieces — including pictures and sounds.

Fig 4 — The same "what comes next?" game works for words and for image patches. Pictures first have to be turned into a short list of discrete patch-codes by a tokenizer (like VQGAN).
  • Images as tokens: a "tokenizer" (for example the one in VQGAN) turns a picture into a grid of codes from a fixed "visual alphabet"; the transformer predicts the codes. The original DALL·E and Google's Parti worked this way.
  • Audio as tokens: neural audio codecs make "sound tokens"; models like AudioLM, VALL-E and MusicLM predict them.
  • Why it matters now: because it's the same machinery as text, one model can read and write words, pictures and sound together (Section 25).
Like finishing a comic strip panel by panel: every new panel has to make sense given all the previous ones. Good for story-following, slower than drawing the whole page at once.

10 Autoencoders & VAEs — Squeeze and Rebuild

An autoencoder has two halves: an encoder that squeezes data into a small latent code, and a decoder that rebuilds the data from the code (Fig 3). Train it to rebuild perfectly and it learns the compact "essence" of the data.

A Variational Autoencoder (VAE) adds one trick: it keeps the latent codes tidy — neatly clustered around a simple bell-curve shape — so you can pick a random point and decode it into something that looks real. That is generation.

Think of a tidy, well-labelled suitcase-packing method: because everyone packs the same orderly way, you can invent a new packing list at random and still unpack a sensible suitcase.
PartJobEveryday picture
EncoderPicture → small codeSummarizing a movie in one sentence
Latent codeThe compact ideaThe sentence
DecoderCode → pictureSomeone re-drawing the movie scene from the sentence
"Variational" tidinessKeeps all codes in a smooth, sampleable shapeAll summaries written in the same tidy format
On its own a VAE makes slightly soft pictures — but as the compressor inside Stable-Diffusion-style models (Section 13) it is everywhere, quietly doing the squeeze and the final "sharpen back to pixels".

11 GANs — The Forger and the Detective

A Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) trains two networks against each other: a generator (the forger) that makes fakes, and a discriminator (the detective) that tries to tell fakes from real examples.

Fig 5 — The detective's feedback teaches the forger; the forger's improving fakes teach the detective. Round after round, the fakes get convincing.
A counterfeiter and a bank inspector in an endless contest: every time the inspector learns a new trick, the counterfeiter has to improve — until the notes are nearly perfect.
Good atTricky about
Very sharp, realistic images in one quick pass (great for real-time use)Training is touchy — one side can overpower the other
Face generation, upscaling, style transfer"Mode collapse": the forger finds a few good fakes and keeps repeating them
Helping other models (adversarial losses in fast "distilled" generators)Harder to steer with text than diffusion models
GANs powered the first wave of deepfake faces; today diffusion and flow models have taken over general image creation, but GAN ideas live on inside fast few-step generators (Section 16).

12 Diffusion — Learn to Clean Up the Noise

Diffusion models are behind most pictures and videos you see today. The idea has two halves.

Fig 6 — Forward: add noise until nothing is left (no learning needed). Reverse: a trained network removes noise a little at a time, guided by your prompt, until a new picture appears.
Imagine a sculptor looking at a rough block of stone and chipping away only what doesn't belong. No single chip creates the statue; hundreds of tiny, well-judged chips do. Each denoising step is one chip.

Why diffusion won

  • Stable to train — no forger-vs-detective tug of war.
  • High quality and variety — different starting noise gives different pictures from the same prompt.
  • Easy to steer — you can nudge each step with text, a sketch, a pose or another image (Section 15 and Part D).
  • Unified view — it connects to "score-based" models (Song et al.) and flow matching (Section 14).
The math, gently (optional) 
Forward (noising), jump straight to step t:
    x_t = sqrt(alpha_bar_t) * x_0  +  sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) * noise

    alpha_bar_t shrinks from ~1 (mostly picture) toward ~0 (mostly noise)

Training: show the network x_t and t; ask it to predict `noise`; reward being close.
Generating: start x_T = pure noise; for t = T ... 1:
    guess the noise -> remove a little of it -> add a pinch of fresh randomness.

You will run exactly this, on a toy problem you can read in one screen, in Level 1.

13 Latent Diffusion & Stable Diffusion — Work in the Small Space

Denoising a full-size picture pixel by pixel is slow and expensive. The breakthrough of latent diffusion (the idea behind Stable Diffusion): do the diffusion inside the small latent space (Section 7) and only expand to pixels at the very end.

Fig 7 — The Stable-Diffusion-style pipeline: (1) understand the words, (2) denoise a small latent guided by them, (3) decode to a full-size picture.

This design made image generation affordable on an ordinary gaming graphics card — and because the weights were released openly, a whole ecosystem of tools, fine-tunes and editors grew around it.

14 Flow Matching & Diffusion Transformers — The Newer Recipe

Two upgrades define the newest image and video models:

14.1 — Flow matching (and "rectified flow")

Instead of learning to undo noise in a wiggly, many-step way, flow matching learns a smooth direction of travel from "noise" to "picture". Rectified flow tries to make that path nearly a straight line — and a straight line needs very few steps.

Fig 8 — Flow models aim for a straighter road from noise to picture, so generation can use fewer steps.

14.2 — Diffusion Transformers (DiT)

Early diffusion models used a "U-Net" (a network shaped like a funnel, good for pictures). Diffusion Transformers swap in the transformer (the same engine as language models) as the denoiser. The picture or video is chopped into patches, like words, and the transformer processes them all with attention. Why this matters: transformers scale — bigger model + more data reliably gives better results — and the same recipe extends from images to video and audio.

U-Net is a specialist craftsperson; the transformer is a flexible team that gets better the more people you hire — and speaks the same language as the text models, so it can read your prompt closely.

Real-world example: Stable Diffusion 3 combines rectified flow with a transformer ("MM-DiT"); several 2026 leaders (image and video) follow the same pattern.

15 Steering the Output — Text, CLIP & Guidance

A model that makes random pictures is a toy. To make your picture it must be steered. Three ingredients do that.

15.1 — Understanding words: CLIP and text encoders

CLIP was trained on hundreds of millions of picture-and-caption pairs so that a photo of a dog and the words "a dog" land near each other on the same map of meaning. That shared map lets a text prompt point at the right region of picture-space. Newer systems use large language models as text encoders for even better understanding of long, detailed prompts.

15.2 — Classifier-free guidance (the "listen harder" knob)

At every denoising step the model makes two guesses: one that listens to your prompt and one that doesn't. Classifier-free guidance pushes the result further in the direction of "the prompt-listening guess minus the ignoring guess". A number called the guidance scale controls how hard.

Fig 9 — The guidance scale trades creativity for obedience. (Many 2026 models pick a good value for you or are trained so it matters less.)

16 Making It Fast — From 50 Steps to 1

Generating with 30–50 denoising steps is slow and costly. A big part of recent progress is doing the same job in very few steps without losing quality.

31 Level 0 — A Text Generator From Scratch

The smallest possible generative model: learn which word tends to follow which, then write by repeatedly picking a plausible next word. That is "learn the pattern, then sample" in about 20 lines.

gen0.py — pure Python, no libraries 
import random

text = "the cat sat on the mat. the dog sat on the log. the cat saw the dog."
words = text.split()

# 1) LEARN: count which word tends to follow which word
follows = {}
for current, nxt in zip(words, words[1:]):
    follows.setdefault(current, []).append(nxt)

# 2) GENERATE: start somewhere, then keep picking a plausible next word
def generate(start="the", length=8, seed=None):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    out = [start]
    for _ in range(length - 1):
        options = follows.get(out[-1])
        if not options:                 # dead end: nothing ever followed this word
            break
        out.append(rng.choice(options))   # pick at random, weighted by how often it was seen
    return " ".join(out)

for seed in range(3):
    print(generate(seed=seed))

# Output from a real run:
#   the log. the cat saw the log. the
#   the mat. the dog sat on the log.
#   the cat sat on the mat. the dog

It never saw "the cat saw the log." — yet it can write it. That's generalizing: recombining learned pieces into something new. A real LLM does the same with far richer memory (a transformer looks at thousands of previous words, not one) and billions of examples.

It's a very forgetful storyteller who only remembers the last word they said. A real language model is a storyteller who remembers the whole story so far.

32 Level 1 — A Tiny Diffusion Model (the mechanics, for real)

Here is diffusion on the smallest imaginable "pictures": single numbers that can only be −2 or +2. We add noise (forward), then generate by starting from pure noise and removing it step by step (reverse). Real models learn the denoiser with a neural network; because our world is so tiny we can write the perfect denoiser by hand, so you see the whole mechanism with nothing hidden.

diff1.py — numpy only 
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# The "training data": every real example is either -2 or +2 (two kinds of "pictures")
data_points = np.array([-2.0, 2.0])

# Noise schedule: how much noise gets added at each of T steps
T = 100
betas = np.linspace(1e-3, 0.05, T)
alphas = 1.0 - betas
alpha_bar = np.cumprod(alphas)           # how much of the original signal survives up to step t

# FORWARD (easy, no learning): jump straight to step t by mixing signal and noise
def add_noise(x0, t):
    noise = rng.standard_normal(x0.shape)
    return np.sqrt(alpha_bar[t]) * x0 + np.sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t]) * noise

# A "denoiser": given a noisy value x_t, guess the clean x0.
# Real models LEARN this with a neural network; here we write the perfect one by hand
# because we know the only two possible clean values.
def predict_clean(x_t, t):
    ab = alpha_bar[t]
    scores = -((x_t[:, None] - np.sqrt(ab) * data_points[None, :]) ** 2) / (2 * (1 - ab))
    weights = np.exp(scores - scores.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    weights /= weights.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return weights @ data_points

# REVERSE (generation): start from pure noise and clean it up one small step at a time
def sample(n):
    x = rng.standard_normal(n)                      # pure noise
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        x0_guess = predict_clean(x, t)
        eps_guess = (x - np.sqrt(alpha_bar[t]) * x0_guess) / np.sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t])
        x = (x - betas[t] / np.sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t]) * eps_guess) / np.sqrt(alphas[t])
        if t > 0:                                   # add a little fresh randomness except at the end
            x += np.sqrt(betas[t]) * rng.standard_normal(n)
    return x

samples = sample(1000)
print("generated samples near -2:", int((samples < 0).sum()), "| near +2:", int((samples >= 0).sum()))
print("example outputs:", np.round(samples[:6], 2))

# Output from a real run:
#   generated samples near -2: 506 | near +2: 494
#   example outputs: [-2.  2. -2.  2.  2.  2.]

Start: 1,000 blobs of random noise. After 100 tiny clean-up steps, every blob has settled onto one of the two real values — about half and half, just like the training data. That is generation: noise in, something that looks like the training data out. A real image model does exactly this, with a neural network guessing "what noise was added?" and each "number" being a million pixels (or a latent, Section 13).

Each blob is a marble dropped onto a bumpy surface with two valleys. Every denoising step is a tiny nudge downhill. Wherever the marble starts, it rolls into one of the valleys — the two "real" values.

33 Level 2 — Text-to-Image With a Real Model (diffusers)

The Hugging Face diffusers library wraps the pipeline of Fig 7 in a few lines. This example uses a one-step "turbo" model (Section 16). Install with pip install diffusers transformers accelerate torch.

import torch

35 Level 4 — Does the Picture Match the Words? (CLIP)

Use CLIP (Section 15) as a judge: given a picture and several descriptions, which description fits best? This is the engine behind CLIPScore (Section 29) and image search.

Score a picture against captions (not run for this page) 
from PIL import Image
from transformers import CLIPModel, CLIPProcessor

model = CLIPModel.from_pretrained("openai/clip-vit-base-patch32")
processor = CLIPProcessor.from_pretrained("openai/clip-vit-base-patch32")

image = Image.open("red_panda.png")          # e.g. the picture from Level 2
texts = ["a red panda reading a book", "a city skyline at night", "a bowl of soup"]

inputs = processor(text=texts, images=image, return_tensors="pt", padding=True)
probs = model(**inputs).logits_per_image.softmax(dim=1)[0]

for text, p in zip(texts, probs):
    print(f"{p:.2f}  {text}")

You should see the first caption score much higher than the others. This is a "discriminative" model judging a "generative" model's work — the teamwork from Section 2. It can still be fooled on counting ("three apples" vs "five apples") and left/right.

42 Model Collapse — When AI Learns From AI

As the internet fills with AI-made text and images, future models may train on them. Researchers showed that repeatedly training on a model's own output makes it forget the rare, interesting parts of the real distribution and drift toward bland averages ("The Curse of Recursion").

Photocopy a photocopy of a photocopy: each generation loses detail and the picture turns to grey mush. Fresh originals keep the quality alive.

43 Responsible-Use Checklist

🙋 Get consent

Don't clone a voice or put a real person's face into generated media without their clear permission.

🏷️ Label it

Say when content is AI-made, especially anything that could be mistaken for real; keep watermarks and credentials intact.

🔍 Verify it

Check facts, names, numbers and anything health, legal or financial before you rely on or publish AI output.

📜 Respect rights

Read licenses; avoid copying living artists' or brands' signature work; keep records of your own creative input.

🔒 Protect data

Don't paste private or confidential information into services that may store or train on it; check settings.

🧑‍⚖️ Keep a human in charge

For decisions that affect people (hiring, grading, medical, legal), a person reviews and takes responsibility.

⚖️ Test for bias

Try prompts across different groups and contexts; fix patterns that stereotype or exclude.

🛑 Have a stop button

Builders: filter inputs and outputs, rate-limit, log, and make it easy to report and remove harmful content.

A simple test before you share anything AI-made: "Would the people in it, and the people seeing it, feel fairly treated if they knew exactly how it was made?"

47 FAQ

Q: Does generative AI copy and paste existing pictures or text?

No — it learns patterns and builds new outputs from them, so most results are genuinely new combinations. Rarely, a model can reproduce something it saw many times (memorization), and that output can still be an infringement or a privacy problem. That's why de-duplication and output checks exist.

Q: Why does the same prompt give different pictures each time?

Generation starts from random noise (or random token choices). A different seed means a different starting point, so a different result. Fix the seed and settings to get the same result again.

Q: Is a bigger model always better?

Often better quality, but also slower and costlier. For many tasks a smaller or "distilled" model is enough. Evaluate on your own tasks rather than assuming.

Q: Diffusion vs GAN vs autoregressive — which is "best"?

None universally. Autoregressive models dominate text and are flexible across modalities; diffusion/flow transformers dominate high-quality images and video; GANs are fast and sharp but harder to train. Real systems mix them (e.g., a VAE compressor + flow transformer + distillation).

Q: Who owns what I generate?

It depends on the tool's terms and your country. In the US, work with no meaningful human authorship generally isn't copyrightable; your creative input (selection, editing, arrangement) can matter. Read the platform terms for commercial use, and ask a lawyer for important cases.

Q: Can I tell whether something is AI-made?

Not reliably by eye or with "AI detector" tools. More dependable: provenance signals (C2PA Content Credentials, invisible watermarks like SynthID) and checking the original source — while remembering missing labels don't prove something is real or fake.

Q: Is it legal to make a voice clone or deepfake of someone?

Without consent it's risky or illegal in many places (fraud, impersonation, defamation, privacy, and specific deepfake and intimate-image laws). With clear consent for a legitimate purpose (dubbing, accessibility) it's generally fine. Rules vary by country; don't do it without permission.

Q: Will generative AI replace artists, writers and coders?

It changes the work: routine drafting and production get faster, while taste, direction, accountability and originality remain human strengths. Impact is uneven and contested, and licensing, consent and credit are active issues. Learning to direct and critique AI output is a valuable skill.

Q: Why do old tutorials and model names stop working?

The field moves very fast: models get retired (even popular products like Sora were reported shut down), libraries release breaking versions, and laws change. Check dates, pin versions, and keep your own prompts and test sets portable.

48 Interview Questions (With Answers) — The Complete Set

48.1 — Fundamentals

Q: What is generative AI, and how does it differ from discriminative AI?

Generative models learn the distribution of data so they can sample new examples (text, images, audio…). Discriminative models learn a boundary or mapping from inputs to labels. Generative answers "what would a new example look like?"; discriminative answers "which class is this?".

Q: What does "learning the distribution" mean?

Capturing how the training examples are spread — which features go together and how often — so that new points sampled from the learned model land in regions that look like real data.

Q: Why can the same prompt produce different outputs?

Generation is stochastic: it begins from random noise (diffusion) or samples tokens from a probability distribution (autoregressive). Seeds fix the randomness; temperature-like settings control how adventurous sampling is (where exposed).

Q: What is a latent space and why use one?

A compressed representation learned by an encoder in which similar items are close. Working there is far cheaper than in pixel space and gives a smooth space for interpolation and editing; latent diffusion exploits this.

Q: Give examples of generative AI across modalities.

Text/code (LLMs), images (diffusion/flow models), video (diffusion transformers over spacetime), speech and music (audio-token or diffusion models), 3D (NeRF, Gaussian splats, text-to-3D), and interactive world models.

Q: Why are LLMs considered generative AI?

They are autoregressive models that generate new token sequences by predicting the next token from the context, producing novel text, code and answers.

48.2 — Model Families

Q: Explain how a VAE works and what its loss has two parts for.

An encoder maps data to a distribution over latents; a decoder reconstructs data from a sampled latent. The loss has a reconstruction term (rebuild accurately) and a KL term (keep latent distributions close to a simple prior so sampling random latents gives sensible outputs). Result: a smooth, sampleable latent space, though raw outputs can be blurry.

Q: Explain the GAN training game and its main failure modes.

A generator maps noise to fake samples; a discriminator classifies real vs fake; each improves against the other. Failure modes: unstable training (one side overpowers), vanishing gradients, and mode collapse (limited output variety). Strengths: sharp samples in a single forward pass.

Q: Why did diffusion models overtake GANs for images?

Stable, simple training (a regression-style denoising objective), better coverage of the data distribution, high fidelity, and easy conditioning (text, images, control maps). The cost, many sampling steps, was reduced via better samplers, distillation and flow-based training.

Q: Compare autoregressive and diffusion generation.

Autoregressive: sequential, one token at a time with exact likelihoods; flexible across modalities but slow for long/high-resolution outputs. Diffusion: iterative refinement of the whole sample in parallel across positions; strong for continuous data like images/video/audio but needs multiple denoising passes. Hybrids exist.

Q: What is flow matching / rectified flow and why is it attractive?

It trains a model to predict the velocity field that transports noise to data along (nearly) straight paths. Training is simple and stable, and straighter paths allow fewer sampling steps; it pairs well with transformers and underlies several recent image/video models.

Q: What changed when diffusion moved from U-Nets to transformers (DiT)?

Treating the latent as patch tokens lets a transformer denoise with attention; it scales predictably with model size and data, shares tooling with LLMs, handles long contexts (video), and takes text conditioning more flexibly.

Q: How can a model generate images autoregressively?

Use a learned tokenizer (e.g., VQGAN-style) to turn images into discrete codes; train a transformer to predict codes in sequence; decode the codes back to pixels. Works naturally alongside text tokens, enabling unified multimodal models.

Q: What is mode collapse versus memorization?

Mode collapse: the model covers too few output varieties (a GAN issue). Memorization: the model reproduces specific training examples. Opposite problems of coverage vs copying; both are evaluated with diversity and nearest-neighbour/regurgitation tests.

48.3 — Diffusion in Depth

Q: Describe the forward and reverse processes of DDPM.

Forward: gradually add Gaussian noise over T steps (closed form x_t = √ᾱ_t x₀ + √(1−ᾱ_t) ε). Reverse: a neural network is trained to predict the noise ε (or x₀ or velocity) from x_t and t; sampling starts at pure noise and applies the learned denoising update step by step, adding a little fresh noise except at the end.

Q: What is the training objective, intuitively?

Pick a random timestep, noise a real sample, ask the network to predict the added noise, and minimize the mean-squared error. It's a simple regression that implicitly learns the score (gradient of the log-density).

Q: What is classifier-free guidance and what does the guidance scale do?

During training the condition (text) is randomly dropped so the model learns both conditional and unconditional predictions. At sampling, the output is extrapolated: unconditional + w × (conditional − unconditional). Larger w improves prompt adherence but reduces diversity and can cause oversaturation/artifacts.

Q: Why do latent diffusion models use a VAE?

The VAE compresses images by a large factor into a latent grid, so the expensive denoiser runs on far fewer values; the VAE decoder reconstructs pixels at the end. This cut compute and memory dramatically and made high-resolution generation practical.

Q: How do you speed up diffusion sampling?

Better solvers/schedulers (fewer, larger steps), distillation (student matches teacher in 1–8 steps), consistency models, adversarial distillation, straighter flow paths, and engineering (quantization, caching, compilation).

Q: How does ControlNet add control without retraining the whole model?

It attaches a trainable copy of the encoder blocks to a frozen base model, fed with a control signal (edges, depth, pose) through zero-initialized connections, so training starts from the base model's behaviour and learns to follow the control map.

Q: What is the role of the seed and the scheduler?

The seed fixes the initial noise (and any injected noise), making runs reproducible. The scheduler defines the noise levels and step update rule; changing it alters speed and the look of results.

48.4 — Modalities

Q: Why is video generation harder than image generation?

Far more data per sample, and it must be consistent across frames (identity, lighting, physics) over time, plus audio sync. Models use spacetime latent patches and diffusion transformers; cost is often per second of video, and long clips are produced by chaining/extending short ones.

Q: How do modern text-to-speech and voice cloning work?

Audio is compressed into tokens with a neural codec; a language-model-style or diffusion model predicts those tokens from text (and a short voice sample for cloning); the codec decodes them to a waveform. A few seconds of reference audio can capture a speaker's timbre — hence consent and misuse concerns.

Q: What is Whisper and what is it good for?

An open speech-recognition model trained on large, diverse weakly-supervised data; robust across accents and noise and usable for multilingual transcription and translation. Larger variants trade speed for accuracy; always proofread names and numbers.

Q: Contrast NeRF, Gaussian splatting and a world model.

NeRF stores a scene in a neural network queried per ray (high quality, slower). Gaussian splatting uses explicit 3-D blobs rendered quickly. A world model generates future frames conditioned on actions without an explicit 3-D scene — flexible and interactive but struggles with long-term consistency.

Q: What are "omni"/any-to-any models and their trade-offs?

One model that ingests and emits several modalities, often by tokenizing everything. Benefits: coherence across modalities, conversational editing, simpler stacks. Costs: scale, evaluation difficulty and broader safety surface (voice + face + text together).

Q: Why is music generation tied up in licensing?

Models learn from recorded music, which is copyrighted; labels sued some generators, and by 2026 several platforms moved to licensed training data and settlements with major labels, with some litigation continuing. The industry is shifting from "train first" to permission-based products.

48.5 — Practice: Prompting, Editing, Customizing, Evaluating

Q: How do you write a good image prompt?

State subject, action, setting, style, light/colour, camera/composition and constraints in clear sentences; change one element at a time and fix the seed to compare; put exact words in quotes for text; describe what you want rather than what you don't.

Q: Inpainting vs image-to-image strength?

Inpainting regenerates only a masked region conditioned on the rest; image-to-image partially noises the whole image and denoises it, with "strength" setting how much changes (low keeps layout, high redraws).

Q: When would you choose LoRA over full fine-tuning or DreamBooth?

LoRA trains small adapter weights on a frozen base: cheap, quick, shareable, stackable — best for adding a style or subject with modest data. Full fine-tuning is costlier and risks forgetting; DreamBooth gives strong subject fidelity but alters more weights.

Q: How would you evaluate an image generator for your product?

Build a private prompt set mirroring real use, include targeted tests (text, counting, hands, safety), run candidates blind with human raters, and track automatic metrics (FID for distribution quality, CLIPScore for prompt match) as secondary signals. Re-run when models change.

Q: Why is FID alone not enough?

It compares feature statistics of whole image sets: it ignores prompt-following, can miss specific failures, depends on the reference set and feature extractor, and doesn't always match human preference.

Q: How do you keep a character consistent across images?

Use reference images or a trained LoRA/subject adapter, fix the seed and model version, reuse a detailed character description, and use conversational-edit or reference-based tools that preserve identity; then review for drift.

48.6 — Coding / Hands-On

Q: Write a tiny generative text model.

Build a dictionary from each word to the list of words that followed it in the training text, then repeatedly choose a random follower of the last word (Level 0). This is a first-order Markov chain — the simplest "learn the pattern, then sample" model.

Q: How do you generate an image with diffusers?

Load a pipeline (e.g., AutoPipelineForText2Image.from_pretrained(model_id)), move it to the GPU, call pipe(prompt=..., num_inference_steps=..., guidance_scale=...) and save .images[0]; set a torch.Generator seed for reproducibility. Distilled "turbo" models need 1–4 steps and little or no guidance.

Q: In the toy diffusion code, why does sampling end up near −2 and +2?

The denoiser predicts the clean value from the noisy one using the only two possible clean values; each reverse step nudges the sample toward whichever is more likely, and noise shrinks to zero, so samples settle onto the two training values in roughly equal proportion.

Q: How would you transcribe audio and then check a generated image matches a caption?

Use a Whisper-based transformers ASR pipeline for the transcript, and CLIP (CLIPModel/CLIPProcessor) to compute image-text similarity over candidate captions, taking the highest softmax score.

48.7 — Risk, Law & Ethics

Q: What are the main risks of generative AI?

Hallucination, bias, privacy leakage and memorization, deepfakes and fraud, non-consensual intimate imagery, copyright disputes, misinformation, creator displacement, energy use, and model collapse from training on synthetic data.

Q: How do watermarking and C2PA differ?

Invisible watermarks embed a hidden signal in the content itself (survives some edits, often readable only by the maker's detector). C2PA attaches cryptographically signed provenance metadata describing origin and edits (tamper-evident, but removable by re-saving or screenshots). They complement each other; neither proves authenticity alone.

Q: What does EU AI Act Article 50 require?

Transparency: tell people when they interact with an AI; providers of generative systems mark synthetic audio, image, video and text in a machine-readable way; deployers disclose deepfakes and AI-generated public-interest text (with an editorial-responsibility exception). Applicable since 2 Aug 2026, with a grace period to 2 Dec 2026 for the marking duty for systems already on the market (as reported).

Q: Summarize the state of copyright and AI training.

Unsettled. Some US rulings treated training as fair use while condemning use of pirated sources (Anthropic settled for $1.5B); other rulings found copying to build a competing tool not fair use; the UK Getty v. Stability ruling rejected the claim that a model is an infringing copy; the NYT v. OpenAI case is pending. Licensing deals are growing. Output copying and human authorship are separate questions.

Q: What is model collapse and how do you avoid it?

Repeated training on model-generated data erodes diversity and rare cases, degrading quality. Avoid by keeping fresh human data in the mix, curating and verifying synthetic data, and using provenance to filter scraped data.

Q: A user asks your app for a realistic video of a named politician endorsing a product. What do you do?

Refuse or restrict: it's impersonation/deepfake with legal and reputational risk. Apply policy filters on prompts and outputs, require consent/verification for likeness use, watermark and label outputs, log for audit, and provide a reporting channel.

48.8 — Advanced, Trends & Scenarios

Q: What are the main trends in generative AI as of late 2026?

Flow-based diffusion transformers as the default for image/video; native audio in video; few-step distilled models; chat-based conversational editing; omni/any-to-any models; world models with better memory; licensed training data and settlements; mandatory content marking (EU); some products being retired or consolidated.

Q: Your product depends on one video model API that gets discontinued. How do you de-risk?

Abstract the model behind an interface, keep prompts/assets/seeds in your own storage, maintain a small evaluation set to compare replacements quickly, keep a second vendor or open-weight fallback, and watch vendor deprecation notices.

Q: Image generation latency is too high for an interactive app. Options?

Use a distilled few-step model, lower resolution then upscale selected results, cache and reuse, batch requests, quantize/compile the model, and show progressive previews.

Q: Design a safe consumer image-generation feature.

Prompt and output safety classifiers, restrictions on real-person likeness and sensitive categories, rate limits and abuse monitoring, watermark + content credentials, clear user terms and reporting, human review for edge cases, logs for audits, and bias testing on representative prompts.

Q: How do text, image and video generation relate in a unified model?

By tokenizing each modality into a shared sequence (or shared latent) and training one transformer to read and write them, the model can answer a spoken question with a picture or turn a script into a narrated video; the same attention machinery scales across modalities.

Q1: What's the difference between generative and discriminative AI?

Discriminative AI sorts or labels things; generative AI makes new things by learning how real data looks.

Given a noisy input (and the step number and prompt), predict the noise so it can be removed — repeated many times to turn pure noise into a picture.

Distillation into a few-step model, and straighter generation paths (flow/rectified flow) or consistency models; also better samplers, quantization and caching.

A watermark is hidden inside the content; credentials are a signed label attached to the file. The watermark survives some edits; the label explains history but can be stripped.
